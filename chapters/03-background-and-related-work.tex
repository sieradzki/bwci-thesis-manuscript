\chapter{Background and Related Work}
\label{chap:related-work}

Research relevant to behavioral work continuity spans work fragmentation and interruption, work continuance, telemetry-based measurement, and temporal and person-dependent validation. These strands provide the conceptual and methodological context for the definition and evaluation of BWCI.

\section{Work Fragmentation, Interruptions, and Resumption}

\subsection{Fragmented Computer-Mediated Work and Task Switching}

Field studies indicate that computer-mediated knowledge work often involves movement among multiple tasks, tools, and broader activity contexts rather than uninterrupted execution of a single task \parencite{gonzalez2004workingSpheres,mark2005fragmentedWork}. González and Mark described these broader contexts as \emph{working spheres}: thematically connected areas of activity that may include multiple events, resources, collaborators, and time horizons. Their observations of analysts, software developers, and managers showed substantial discontinuity in the execution of activities and frequent movement among tools, tasks, and working spheres. These findings establish fragmentation as an observable characteristic of the studied work settings, but they do not provide a universal switching rate for knowledge work.

An application, a task, and a working sphere represent different levels of analysis. A working sphere may involve several documents, software tools, and physical resources, while applications such as email or scheduling tools may be shared among different working spheres. Czerwinski et al. likewise found that participants described task switches at different levels of granularity, and the study discussed the need to organize project-related resources across applications. Application-level telemetry therefore records changes in the observed tool context but does not by itself identify semantic task or working-sphere boundaries \parencite{gonzalez2004workingSpheres,czerwinski2004diary}.

In a detailed observational study of 24 information workers, \textcite{mark2005fragmentedWork} found that 57.1\% of central and peripheral working-sphere segments were classified as interrupted; the analysis considered only interruptions that took participants outside the current working sphere. After interruptions during the final work hour were excluded, 77.2\% of interrupted work was resumed the same day. Among these same-day resumptions, participants engaged in an average of 2.26 intervening working spheres before returning. Fragmentation could therefore involve a sequence of other activities rather than immediate alternation between two tasks. These estimates were obtained in a specific technology-intensive organizational setting and should not be treated as universal rates.

A one-week diary study by \textcite{czerwinski2004diary} recruited 11 experienced Windows users, with one participant excluded from the main analyses for not meeting the stated task-switching criterion. Among the remaining participants, self-initiated switches were the largest recorded category and accounted for 40\% of task switches. Tasks to which participants later returned were longer, involved more documents, and were rated as more difficult to resume than other tasks. The findings distinguish self-directed task management from switches imposed by external interruptions.

The role of self-interruption was examined further by \textcite{dabbish2011selfInterruption}, who analyzed 889 hours of observed task-switching behavior from 36 information workers across three high-technology organizations. Variation in self-interruption was associated with organizational environment, individual differences, and previously experienced external interruptions; participants working in open-office environments self-interrupted at a higher rate. Because the study was observational, these relationships do not establish causal effects.

Taken together, these studies support treating continuity and fragmentation as properties of observable work organization while preserving a strict interpretation boundary. A transition may reflect an interruption, but it may also result from coordination, deliberate reprioritization, self-directed task management, or resumption of ongoing work. The presence of a transition therefore does not by itself establish disruption or an adverse task outcome.

\subsection{Interruption Costs, Resumption, and Attention Residue}

Even when a transition constitutes an interruption rather than ordinary task progression, its consequences depend on when it occurs, how the interrupted task is structured, and which outcome is used to define its cost. Controlled studies have therefore examined several distinct consequences, including completion time, errors, affective responses, and the delay before observable task resumption.

The interruption literature itself contains substantial terminological variation. The integrative review of 247 publications by \textcite{puranik2020interruptionReview} documents inconsistencies in how work interruptions and closely related concepts such as task interruption, distraction, disruption, and intrusion have been defined and studied. Consequently, these terms should not be treated as interchangeable solely because they describe changes in ongoing work. Comparisons with breaks, multitasking, or task transitions likewise require preserving each study's operational definition rather than assuming construct equivalence.

In a laboratory experiment, \textcite{bailey2006attention} compared peripheral tasks presented during primary-task execution with the same peripheral tasks presented at coarse task boundaries. Interruptions during execution increased completion time and errors and produced greater annoyance and anxiety. Within this experiment, interruption timing affected both performance and affective outcomes. The result does not show that application switches in natural computer use identify equivalent task boundaries.

Task structure also affects the operational cost assigned to an interruption. \textcite{iqbal2006interruptionCost} interrupted participants at selected subtask boundaries and defined resumption lag as the interval from closing the peripheral-task window to the first observable action in the resumed primary task. Structural characteristics of the boundary were informative about this outcome, and the resulting model was subsequently evaluated with a different set of laboratory tasks. The study supports distinguishing interruption moments according to the structure of a goal-directed task. Its explicitly modeled subtask boundaries, however, are not interchangeable with application switches or other passive telemetry events.

A different pattern was observed by \textcite{mark2008cost}. In the interruption conditions, participants spent less net time on the primary email task after interruption time was subtracted. Error counts and politeness scores did not differ significantly across conditions, although mean message length showed an overall condition effect and was greatest without interruptions. Participants also reported greater stress, frustration, time pressure, and effort. This combination shows why interruption effects cannot be inferred from net primary-task time or these limited output measures alone. It also cautions against treating interruptions as producing a uniform decline in task performance, because behavioral compensation and subjective workload may move in different directions.

A related account is provided by \textcite{leroy2009attentionResidue}: switching away from unfinished work may leave part of a person's attention associated with the previous task and may impair performance on the subsequent task. This evidence does not imply that every task switch, interruption, or application transition produces attention residue.

Behavioral resumption, resumption of task performance, and cognitive recovery are related but distinct concepts. \emph{Behavioral resumption} is an observable return to activity, such as reopening an application or producing a new input event. \emph{Resumption of task performance} requires task-specific evidence that effective execution has been restored. A resumption lag ending at the first observable action quantifies one aspect of the return process, but does not by itself establish restored performance. \emph{Cognitive recovery} concerns an internal state that computer telemetry does not directly observe. Evidence from controlled experiments with explicitly defined tasks and interruptions does not transfer automatically to naturalistic interaction logs. Interruption costs depend on timing, task structure, context, and the outcome measured; observable transitions and returns to activity therefore cannot be assigned a universal disruption cost or treated as direct evidence of cognitive recovery.

\section{Interruptibility and Work-Continuance Modeling}

\subsection{Sensing Current Interruptibility}

Research on current interruptibility asks whether a particular moment appears suitable for an interruption. Fogarty et al. and Züger et al. operationalized the construct primarily through contemporaneous participant ratings \parencite{fogarty2005interruptibility,zueger2018sensing}. This differs from measuring the cost of an interruption that actually occurs: the former concerns perceived appropriateness at the current moment, whereas the latter concerns consequences such as delay, errors, or workload.

An early field investigation by \textcite{fogarty2005interruptibility} examined whether current interruptibility could be estimated from sensed office context. Four university staff members with similar administrative responsibilities were observed in their normal offices for 14--22 working days, producing 602 hours of recordings. Participants were prompted to rate their current interruptibility on a five-point scale. Target construction was not purely self-reported: 54 prompts that participants clearly heard but did not answer within 30 seconds were assigned to the ``Highly Non-interruptible'' category after individual inspection of the recordings. Audio and video were manually coded to simulate plausible sensors representing social interaction, telephone use, posture, office occupancy, keyboard and mouse activity, and other contextual events. Models based on these manually simulated sensor values exceeded a constant majority-class baseline, and the best reported two-class model outperformed human observers judging the same recordings. Separate analyses examined limited automated audio sensing and hybrid models. The study provided early evidence for sensor-based estimation of current interruptibility, but its four-person, occupationally homogeneous sample and extensive reliance on manually simulated sensors do not support broad population generalization. 

Psychophysiological sensing had also been investigated as a means of estimating perceived interruptibility during software-development work \parencite{zueger2015interruptibility}. Extending this line into a longer naturalistic setting, \textcite{zueger2018sensing} conducted a two-week field study with 13 professional software developers from three companies. Participants periodically rated their current interruptibility on a seven-point scale. Predictor streams included keyboard and mouse interaction, the active application window, calendar information, heart-related measurements, physical activity, and sleep. The comparison of computer-interaction, biometric, and combined feature sets was conducted using participant-specific two-state models. In this comparison, computer-interaction data yielded higher reported accuracy than biometric data alone, while the combined feature set produced the strongest reported result.

A related technical precedent is FlowLight, an automatic interruptibility indicator based mainly on recent keyboard and mouse activity relative to a user's own activity history, supplemented by calendar and presence information \parencite{zueger2017flowlight}. Its combined software and physical-light system was evaluated in a long-term field study involving 449 knowledge workers at 15 sites in 12 countries, all within one multinational corporation. The activity heuristics were revised during deployment in response to user feedback, and the authors noted that the inferred status could be inaccurate for activities such as reading or thinking. FlowLight therefore provides a precedent for person-relative telemetry-based interruptibility indication, although its construct and intervention outcomes differ from those examined for BWCI.

Züger et al. also distinguished participant-specific modeling from transfer to a held-out participant. Participant-specific models learned from labeled observations of the same person, whereas a leave-one-participant-out analysis trained on the remaining participants and evaluated transfer to a person excluded from model fitting. Participant-specific models performed better for most participants, but the two analyses represent different intended uses rather than interchangeable estimates of one predictive capability.

In the studies by Fogarty et al. and Züger et al., target construction relied primarily on contemporaneous participant ratings. Such ratings provide a reference independent of the sensor features, but remain subjective operationalizations shaped by how respondents understand interruptibility and the hypothetical interruption being considered. They are not objective ground truth for interruption cost, psychological focus, or a universal internal state. Keyboard, mouse, application, contextual, and biometric signals served as predictors of the ratings rather than direct measurements of those constructs.

Reported accuracy from these studies is not directly comparable with classification performance reported for BWCI because the targets, dichotomization rules, class prevalence, populations, and validation procedures differ. Classification of current interruptibility estimates a reported state at the sampling moment, whereas BWCI concerns prospective prediction of the future level and operationally defined deterioration of a bounded behavioral proxy. Work-continuance modeling introduces a further question: how long the current condition is expected to persist.

\subsection{Predicting Uninterruptible Duration and Work Continuance}

Current interruptibility estimation concerns whether an interruption appears appropriate at a particular moment. A related prospective question is how long an operationally identified interval of lower estimated interruptibility is expected to persist during ongoing computer-mediated work.

An early study by \textcite{iga2015uninterruptibleDuration} examined uninterruptible duration using long-term PC-operation records from four participants. It related duration to application category, keyboard--mouse balance, keyboard and mouse activity rates, application-switching frequency, and time of day. The study provides an early example of duration-oriented analysis using closely related families of computer-interaction signals.

\textcite{shiratori2020workContinuance} developed this direction using naturalistic PC-use records from 13 faculty members and students in a university setting, with individual recording periods extending over several months. The probabilistic work-continuance model represented the probability that work would continue as a function of four feature families: application type, PC-operation activity, the balance between keyboard and mouse activity, and application-switching frequency. These features represented observable characteristics of the current computer-use context and did not require physiological sensing.

Notably, the derived uninterruptible duration tended to increase as application-switching frequency increased. Participant-level correlations were positive for all 13 participants, ranging from 0.07 to 0.46. The authors proposed that concentrated work on a task requiring multiple applications could involve frequent switching between those applications. This result does not establish that switching is beneficial, but it directly cautions against treating fewer application switches as a universally validated indicator of stronger work continuity \parencite{shiratori2020workContinuance}.

The prediction target was derived neither from direct participant reports nor from independently observed interruption events. A separate real-time estimator based on PC-operation information assigned interruptibility to three levels. An \emph{uninterruptible duration} began when the estimated interruptibility level changed from high to medium and ended when it returned to high. Its boundaries were therefore generated algorithmically from related computer telemetry. The authors reported limited classification precision for the underlying estimator in both its three-level and two-level forms and noted that the derived duration could differ substantially from interruptibility as experienced by the participant. The resulting target is consequently best interpreted as an operationally identified work-continuance interval rather than as objective evidence of experienced interruptibility \parencite{shiratori2020workContinuance}.

Evaluation included leave-one-participant-out cross-validation, with positive correlations between predicted and derived durations reported for every held-out participant, although prediction errors varied between individuals. To approximate prediction during an ongoing interval, the authors also used only information available three minutes after its onset. The association remained positive but became weaker, and prediction error increased relative to the full-interval analysis \parencite{shiratori2020workContinuance}.

The study also documents important instrumentation limits. Work performed away from the monitored computer or on an additional device was not represented reliably. A task change involving only a switch between windows of the same application could remain unobserved, while external events such as meetings or visitors could end an interval independently of the recorded PC activity. Substantial errors remained for individual durations. Together with the university-based sample, these limitations constrain the interpretation and generalization of the reported relationships \parencite{shiratori2020workContinuance}.

The study is closely related to BWCI through its use of keyboard and mouse activity, application context, and switching behavior, including a prospective duration-prediction variant. It also evaluates transfer through a leave-one-participant-out procedure. The prediction target and temporal estimand are nevertheless different. Shiratori et al. estimate time to the end of an algorithmically identified uninterruptible interval, whereas BWCI models the future level or operationally defined deterioration of an explicitly bounded behavioral-continuity proxy. Similar predictor families therefore do not imply that the two approaches measure the same construct. These contrasts also raise the broader question of how interruptibility and continuance models behave across individuals and how replication evidence from the wider literature should inform interpretation of small-sample results.

\subsection{User Dependence and Replication Evidence}
Studies of interruptibility and work continuance have reported substantial variation in model performance and participant-level prediction errors. Within-participant prediction using labeled data from the same participant, transfer to a participant excluded from model fitting, and adaptation using participant-specific data therefore represent different evaluation scenarios rather than interchangeable measures of predictive quality. 

This distinction was explicit in the field study by \textcite{zueger2018sensing}. Participant-specific interruptibility models were trained separately for each developer, whereas a general model was evaluated by training on the remaining participants and testing on the held-out person. Participant-specific models performed better for most participants, but not all, and model performance varied across individuals. Learning-curve analyses further showed that the amount of labeled personal data required for an individual model to approach or exceed the general model was participant-dependent. These findings provide evidence of person-dependent predictive relationships, but do not establish that personalization necessarily improves every model or every dimension of predictive performance \parencite{zueger2018sensing}.

Person dependence was also visible in the work-continuance analysis of \textcite{shiratori2020workContinuance}. Although leave-one-participant-out evaluation produced positive correlations between predicted and derived durations for every held-out participant, prediction errors differed considerably between individuals. The authors discussed differences in working style, including keyboard- or mouse-dominant operation and window-management preferences, as possible explanations for this variability rather than as formally established causes \parencite{shiratori2020workContinuance}.

Replication evidence provides a separate reason for caution when interpreting performance from small studies. \textcite{poreba2026replication} conducted an external close replication of the laboratory study reported by \textcite{zueger2015interruptibility}, while documenting deviations from the original study design and procedure. Ten participants completed 60-minute programming sessions, yielding 82 interruptibility observations. The replication retained the five-state perceived-interruptibility target and its binary transformation, used a Naive Bayes classifier, and evaluated accuracy using 10 repetitions of 10-fold cross-validation against a majority-class ZeroR baseline. For the binary target, accuracy was 8.8 percentage points above the baseline, but variability across validation runs was high and the advantage was not statistically significant. For the five-state target, accuracy was below the majority baseline \parencite{poreba2026replication}.

Poreba and Sobernig identified small sample size, class imbalance, and the ad hoc binary transformation of the five-point rating as important threats to validity. The replication's binary label distribution was substantially more balanced than in the original laboratory study, while the five-state label distributions remained imbalanced in both studies. The authors also documented methodological deviations from the original experiment. The replication therefore shows that the apparent strength of predictive results can be sensitive to sample composition, target construction, validation variability, and the baseline used for comparison \parencite{poreba2026replication}.

Taken together, prior work provided evidence that current interruptibility and prospective work continuance can be modeled, while also documenting person-dependent performance and limits on broad generalization from small samples. This evidence supports separate evaluation of within-participant prediction using labeled data from the same participant, transfer to a previously unseen participant, and personalization, together with explicit baselines and attention to participant-level heterogeneity and uncertainty. Because BWCI concerns a different behavioral target, these studies inform its evaluation without establishing equivalence with interruptibility or work continuance.

\section{Behavioral Telemetry and Proxy Measurement}

\subsection{Computer-Interaction Telemetry and Logs-Based Measures}

Computer-interaction telemetry provides observable records of activity at several levels of abstraction. At the lowest level, a system may record interaction events or aggregate statistics such as keystrokes, mouse actions, active-application use, application switches, or periods without input. These observations have direct technical meaning, such as indicating that an input event occurred or that a particular application was active, but they do not themselves specify the task being performed or the broader significance of the behavior. Additional interpretive meaning is introduced when events are transformed into representations such as activity rhythms, application-context sequences, or operationally defined work sessions.

BEHACOM illustrates a relatively low-level aggregated representation of computer interaction. \textcite{sanchez2020behacom} collected naturalistic computer-use data from twelve users over 55 consecutive days without prescribed tasks. All participants were right-handed men aged 20--45; ten were Spanish and two were Italian, and the amount of recorded data differed substantially among users. These characteristics constrain demographic and population-level generalization. The released dataset represents keyboard, mouse, application-usage, and selected system-resource usage through features aggregated in nominal one-minute windows. Sensitive raw data were processed locally, did not leave the monitored device, and were deleted after feature extraction. The release does not retain full typed strings or raw event sequences, but it contains detailed key-, digraph-, timing-, application-, and system-derived summaries. The records are associated with user identifiers and are not accompanied by independently observed interruption, focus, or task-performance outcomes. BEHACOM consequently provides a rich substrate for constructing behavioral representations, but the semantic interpretation of those representations must be supplied by a separate operational definition.

Other systems move closer to task-level representations by logging interactions across work tools. \textcite{chen2022workgraph}, for example, used anonymized logs from internal engineering tools to define \emph{personal-focus sessions}. Their operationalization distinguished activities classified as personal focus from interruption events such as work-chat, calendar, or notification interactions. This approach provides an example of how cross-tool logs can be transformed into higher-level work-session constructs, while the resulting meaning depends on the rules used to classify activities and interruptions.

A more explicit examination of this measurement step was provided by \textcite{brown2023focusedWork}. Their logs represented actions from multiple development and communication tools as time-ordered tool-action events; the metric itself relied on tool and action labels rather than user-generated textual content or other metadata. Related actions were represented through learned event similarities and combined into a logs-based \emph{focus time} metric. Diary reports from 51 developers were used both to assess session-level agreement and to select the metric's time-window and similarity-threshold parameters from a grid of 480 candidate settings. A separate quarterly survey analysis involving 13,383 engineers then related longer-term focus-time summaries to self-reported focus or flow. That analysis produced mixed results: the number of focus sessions and the proportion of active days containing a focus session were positively associated with the self-report item, whereas total focus hours and the proportion of active time classified as focus were not.

Importantly, Brown et al.'s preliminary diary study found that nearly identical patterns of logged behavior could be described as flow or non-flow depending on the participant's retrospective experience. This observation contributed to a shift from attempting to identify flow itself toward the broader concept of focused work, which the authors treated as related to, but not equivalent to, flow \parencite{brown2023focusedWork}.

Brown et al. therefore provide a practical example of the distinction between an observable event, a constructed behavioral representation, and an interpretive construct. Tool actions and their temporal relationships are observed; event similarities and focus sessions are constructed from those observations; the interpretation of those sessions as focused work additionally relies on the operational definition and self-report evidence with different roles in metric development and evaluation \parencite{brown2023focusedWork}. The same distinction applies more generally to computer-interaction measures. BWCI belongs to this broad family of constructed behavioral representations derived from telemetry, but its meaning cannot follow from keyboard, mouse, or application events alone. Defining such a representation therefore raises a separate measurement question: which construct is supported by the chosen operationalization and by the evidence used to evaluate it?

\subsection{Operationalization and Construct Validity}
The scientific interpretation of a computer-interaction measure depends on its operational definition and on the inferences drawn from it. Observable indicators such as input activity or application switching can be combined into a score or behavioral representation, but interpreting that representation may involve claims that extend beyond what is directly recorded. The distinction between an observed indicator, its operationalization, and the broader construct attributed to it is therefore central to interpreting behavioral telemetry.

The classical account of construct validity by \textcite{cronbach1955constructValidity} addresses this problem for measurements interpreted in terms of attributes that are not directly observed. Cronbach and Meehl argued that construct validation requires connecting a proposed interpretation to a wider network of theoretically expected relationships involving observable evidence. A favorable correlation, prediction, or experimental result may contribute evidence, but construct validity depends on a broader pattern of theoretically expected relationships rather than on any single empirical result.

The same distinction applies to computational measurement. \textcite{jacobs2021measurement} describe theoretical constructs as abstractions that cannot be observed directly and must instead be operationalized through observable properties assumed to be related to them. Operationalization necessarily introduces assumptions and design choices about which indicators are relevant and how they represent the intended construct. A mismatch can consequently arise between the theoretical phenomenon that a computational system is said to measure and what its operational definition actually captures. Making those assumptions explicit is necessary for evaluating the resulting interpretation.

Some composite measures are not intended as reflective scales in which observed indicators are interchangeable manifestations of a single latent variable. BWCI is best treated as a design-based composite index rather than as a reflective scale of an assumed latent variable. Its value is determined by explicit transformations and aggregation of selected behavioral components. Those components define the operational index rather than serving as interchangeable manifestations of an independently identified latent construct. This use of a composite does not specify a causal formative latent-variable measurement model \parencite{bollen2011threeCs,diamantopoulos2001formativeIndex}. Accordingly, high internal consistency among the components is not a necessary validity criterion. More relevant considerations include substantive coverage and the direction assigned to the components, dependence among indicators, weighting and aggregation rules, robustness to alternative specifications, and relationships with evidence external to the index definition \parencite{oecd2008compositeIndicators}.

Equal weighting is a transparent design convention, not an assumption-free choice. Equal nominal coefficients at the component level do not ensure equal effective influence of the underlying signals: differences in variability, dependence among indicators, and algebraic reuse of the same input can overweight or double count information. Equal weights should therefore be reported as part of the operational definition and examined through sensitivity analysis, rather than treated as empirical evidence that the components contribute equally \parencite{oecd2008compositeIndicators}.

The logs-based study by \textcite{brown2023focusedWork}, discussed above, provides a practical illustration of the distinction between development and validation evidence. Diary reports informed both the interpretation and parameter selection of the metric. The mixed quarterly-survey associations therefore constitute the more distinct, although still limited, source of validation evidence.

BWCI is a constructed, design-based behavioral work-continuity proxy derived from observable computer-interaction telemetry. The empirical inputs comprise recorded telemetry and features computed from it; continuity is the property defined by the specified transformations and aggregation rule. The term \emph{proxy} reflects this restricted inferential role: the index summarizes specified aspects of observed behavior without asserting that a broader state is directly observed. Consequently, BWCI is not, by its definition alone, a direct measure of psychological focus, attention, productivity, work quality, perceived interruptibility, or a ground-truth interruption.

This boundary is particularly important when interpreting predictive performance. BWCI is constructed from computer-interaction telemetry, while models predicting future BWCI use current and historical information derived from the same broad measurement substrate. Successful prospective prediction is meaningful evidence that the operationalized behavioral outcome contains temporally predictable structure. It is not independent evidence that the outcome corresponds to a broader construct that is not directly observed in the telemetry. This distinction separates valid prediction of a constructed outcome from independent evidence about what that outcome represents.

Because the deterioration target is defined as future BWCI minus current BWCI, a predictor that includes current BWCI is mathematically coupled to the target through their shared term. Since BWCI is bounded in $[0,1]$, deterioration of at least $0.15$ is algebraically impossible when current BWCI is below $0.15$; the current value therefore contains deterministic information about the support of the deterioration outcome. This does not constitute look-ahead leakage when the current value is available at prediction time. Whether the hard support constraint affects a particular evaluation depends on the observed range of current BWCI, while the shared-term coupling remains a property of the target definition. Separately, change-score analyses can be affected by regression to the mean \parencite{tu2007changeInitialValue}. Future-level prediction and a current-value-only baseline provide important context for assessing incremental signal beyond the current index level, although they do not remove the coupling or bounded-score geometry.

Stronger interpretations require evidence beyond the score definition and its internal predictability. Depending on the proposed inference, relevant evidence for a telemetry-based proxy may include relationships with independently collected participant reports, experimentally manipulated conditions, task outcomes, or other observations expected to show theoretically meaningful agreement or divergence \parencite{cronbach1955constructValidity,jacobs2021measurement}. Such evidence may support, refine, or challenge the interpretation attached to an operational measure. An explicit operational definition therefore clarifies the inferential boundary of a behavioral proxy, but neither the definition itself nor its predictability settles its broader meaning. This distinction shifts attention to the limits of the computer-interaction observations from which the representation is constructed.

\subsection{Data and Interpretive Boundaries}
Computer-interaction telemetry is necessarily selective: it records activity visible to the instrumented devices and software rather than the full set of activities that may constitute work. A period without recorded keyboard or mouse input establishes only the absence of those observed input events during the interval. It does not distinguish among activities compatible with little or no input, such as reading, planning, conversations, or meetings, work performed away from the monitored computer, genuine inactivity, or limitations in observation. In their field reconstruction of task suspension and resumption, \textcite{iqbal2007taskRecovery} likewise noted that users can remain active in a task without producing computer input, and FlowLight's authors reported analogous errors for reading and thinking \parencite{zueger2017flowlight}. Assigning any one meaning to an input gap therefore requires information beyond the absence of input itself.

Application telemetry has a related boundary. An active application or an application-switch event provides information about the observed tool context, but does not uniquely identify the task being performed, the relevance of that application to the task, or the significance of a transition. A task may change without a change of application, and an application transition may occur while the same broader task continues. These limitations were visible in the naturalistic study by \textcite{shiratori2020workContinuance}. Switching between separate windows of the same application was not represented by their application-switch measure, activity on an additional computer was absent from the primary-PC record, and meetings, visitors, and other external events could affect work independently of the monitored PC activity. Computer-only sensing therefore provides only a partial view of the context in which observed interaction occurs.

Ambiguity can remain even when a richer pattern of events is available. As discussed previously, \textcite{brown2023focusedWork} found that nearly identical patterns of logged activity could accompany retrospective reports of either flow or non-flow. The observation does not imply that logs are uninformative; rather, it shows that similarity in recorded behavior does not uniquely determine the subjective meaning associated with that behavior.

Minimizing the retention of content-level data limits the explicit content preserved, but does not make interaction telemetry inherently anonymous or non-sensitive. BEHACOM, for example, aggregated locally collected raw interaction data into behavioral feature vectors and transmitted those vectors to the server. During server-side dataset generation, a user identifier was appended to each received vector, while the raw data were deleted locally after transmission \parencite{sanchez2020behacom}. Although full typed strings and raw event sequences are not retained in the released representation, detailed derived interaction summaries remain. Research on behavioral signals shows that information may remain in timing and interaction dynamics themselves. \textcite{balagani2018contextPrivacy} showed, in the setting of continuous keystroke authentication, that keystroke latencies could reveal which application was being used for text entry. Separately, \textcite{zheng2011mouseVerification} demonstrated that fine-grained mouse-movement dynamics could support user verification. These results concern representations and experimental settings different from BWCI; they do not establish that BWCI features reveal application context or permit user identification. They do show more generally that content-minimized interaction telemetry can remain behaviorally distinctive and potentially sensitive.

Privacy protection during collection does not settle the ethics of workplace deployment. A meta-analysis of 94 independent samples found no evidence that electronic performance monitoring improved worker performance, while monitoring was associated with greater worker stress; more transparent and less invasive monitoring was associated with more positive worker attitudes \parencite{ravid2023monitoring}. These findings caution against treating privacy-preserving data collection as sufficient justification for workplace deployment. BWCI is not interpreted in this thesis as an employee-performance score. Purpose limitation, worker involvement, transparency, contestability, and controls on secondary use are treated here as normative safeguards for any operational deployment rather than as empirical findings established by the meta-analysis.

These observational boundaries constrain the interpretation of any representation constructed from such data. For BWCI, absence of input cannot be equated with absence of work, application-context stability cannot be equated with semantic task stability, and recorded interaction cannot represent activity occurring outside the monitored computer. Similar telemetry may also admit different experiential interpretations, while content minimization does not eliminate the need to treat behavioral data as potentially sensitive. These limitations do not make computer-interaction telemetry unsuitable for behavioral measurement; they specify what conclusions its evidence can support.

Taken together, computer-interaction logs provide useful evidence about observable behavior, but behavioral representations require explicit operational definitions, broader construct interpretations require evidence beyond the representation itself, and the instrumentation determines what remains observable or ambiguous. Accordingly, BWCI is interpreted as a proxy of continuity in recorded computer interaction rather than as a direct measure of a broader work or psychological state.

\section{Evaluation Principles for Temporal and Person-Dependent Prediction}

Model performance is meaningful only relative to the prediction scenario represented by the evaluation design. This is particularly important when observations are ordered in time and multiple records originate from the same individuals. A procedure that estimates performance on later observations from already represented participants addresses a different question from one that estimates transfer to a participant absent from model development.

For prospective prediction, the direction of evaluation should correspond to the direction of the intended inference. \textcite{saeb2017useCase} make this point in the different domain of clinical and mobile-sensing prediction: the appropriate validation scheme depends on the use case, and prediction of future states for an already observed person can be evaluated by separating that person's records in time. Random record-wise splitting instead mixes repeated records across partitions and therefore does not by itself establish either later-time prediction or transfer to a new participant. In temporal behavioral data, the relevant question is consequently whether information available before a prediction point supports prediction of a later outcome. Incorporating a longer history through lagged features or a sequence representation changes the information supplied to the model, but does not by itself establish superior prediction relative to a simpler representation.

Person-level generalization introduces a separate distinction. When records from a participant are available during model development, evaluation on that participant's later observations can exploit stable person-specific regularities. Unseen-participant evaluation instead asks whether relationships learned from other participants transfer without prior data from the target person. Personalization or adaptation asks a third question because observations from the target participant are deliberately introduced into model fitting or adaptation. Saeb et al. show why record-wise and subject-wise validation can yield substantially different estimates when repeated person-level observations are present, while the interruptibility study by \textcite{zueger2018sensing} provides a domain-specific example in which participant-specific and held-out-participant models produced different performance patterns \parencite{saeb2017useCase}. None of these scenarios provides an interchangeable estimate of a single notion of general model quality, and personalization should be evaluated rather than assumed to improve prediction.

Repeated observations nested within participants allow associations to be examined at two distinct levels: within individuals over time and between individuals. These intraindividual and interindividual associations need not agree \parencite{fisher2018groupIndividual}. This issue is distinct from predictive transfer: held-out-participant evaluation asks whether a fitted prediction rule generalizes to a person excluded from model development, while personalization deliberately introduces information from that person \parencite{saeb2017useCase,zueger2018sensing}. Claims about behavioral change within individuals should therefore be supported by within-person analyses, while transfer to a held-out participant should be reported as a separate estimand.

The performance measure must likewise reflect the target distribution and the property of interest. \textcite{saito2015precisionRecall} show that, under class imbalance, accuracy may conceal poor performance on the minority class and that ROC-space can obscure practically important changes that become visible in precision--recall space. Precision is directly affected by the number of false positive predictions relative to true positive predictions, making the precision--recall perspective particularly informative when the positive outcome is uncommon. This supports evaluating rare-outcome ranking with measures that emphasize the positive class while reporting prevalence and threshold-dependent behavior separately. Selecting a particular scalar summary of the precision--recall curve remains a separate methodological decision, specified in Chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}.

Ranking, probability calibration, and thresholded decisions address further distinct questions. Ranking or discrimination concerns whether cases with the positive outcome tend to receive higher model scores than negative cases. Calibration concerns whether predicted probabilities correspond to observed outcome frequencies. \textcite{niculescu2005goodProbabilities} demonstrate empirically that strong classification accuracy or ROC-area performance does not guarantee well-calibrated probability estimates, and that the quality of predicted probabilities can therefore require separate evaluation. Converting a score or probability into a binary decision introduces an additional operating threshold: performance at that point depends not only on the ordering or calibration of predictions, but also on where the threshold is placed. A ranking metric, a calibration assessment, and threshold-specific classification results therefore characterize different properties of the same predictive system rather than substitutes for one another.

The evaluation of BWCI addresses several separate questions: prospective prediction of later proxy values for represented participants, transfer to a held-out participant, adaptation using participant-specific information, ranking of a deterioration outcome, probability calibration, and classification at a chosen operating threshold. These questions define distinct estimands and predictive properties whose interpretation depends on the temporal setting, the relationship between training and evaluation participants, the class distribution, and the form in which model output is evaluated.

\section{Synthesis and Positioning of BWCI}

\subsection{Comparison with Closely Related Approaches}

Closely related research spans sensing current interruptibility, constructing automatic availability indicators, reconstructing task suspension and resumption, predicting work continuance or transition opportunities, and constructing higher-level behavioral measures from computer-interaction logs. These lines use overlapping sources of behavioral evidence, but differ in the construct being operationalized, the provenance of the prediction target, and the temporal question being addressed.

Current-interruptibility research estimates whether an interruption appears appropriate at the present moment. Fogarty et al. and Züger et al. primarily operationalized this construct through contemporaneous participant ratings, while using sensed behavioral, contextual, or physiological information as predictors \parencite{fogarty2005interruptibility,zueger2018sensing}. FlowLight instead deployed a person-relative, telemetry-derived status as part of an intervention and explicitly documented activities for which the automatic state could be inaccurate \parencite{zueger2017flowlight}. The earlier laboratory work by Züger and Fritz belongs to the same general line, and its later close replication by Poreba and Sobernig illustrates how small samples, target construction, class prevalence, and baseline choice can affect the apparent strength of predictive results \parencite{zueger2015interruptibility,poreba2026replication}. These approaches concern perceived or operational current interruptibility rather than continuity in the observed computer-interaction pattern itself.

A second line asks how long an ongoing condition is expected to persist. Iga et al. provide an early duration-oriented example based on PC-operation features \parencite{iga2015uninterruptibleDuration}. Shiratori et al. subsequently modeled the duration of intervals derived from an operational interruptibility estimator and evaluated prediction across participants \parencite{shiratori2020workContinuance}. This work is particularly close to BWCI in its use of keyboard, mouse, application, and switching information and in its prospective orientation. Its target is nevertheless different: it estimates time to the end of an algorithmically identified interval rather than the future value or deterioration of a continuously defined behavioral proxy.

A related line examines task suspension, resumption, and candidate actions to continue, change tasks, or take a break. Iqbal and Horvitz reconstructed a disruption-and-recovery lifecycle anchored in incoming email and instant-messaging alerts, using application and window activity together with alert logs and explicit heuristics, while Kaur et al. modeled action-specific expected outcomes for those three alternatives using affect, workstation activity, and task information \parencite{iqbal2007taskRecovery,kaur2020transitionsBreaks}. These approaches are not equivalent to BWCI, but they are close comparators because they operationalize transitions or continuance without using BWCI's composite-score definition.

Another line constructs higher-level representations directly from work logs. Workgraph operationalized personal-focus sessions through relationships among events recorded across engineering tools \parencite{chen2022workgraph}. Brown et al. similarly constructed a logs-based focused-work metric from cross-tool activity. Diary reports informed parameter selection, while a separate quarterly survey analysis yielded mixed associations across alternative focus-time summaries \parencite{brown2023focusedWork}. These studies demonstrate that computer logs can support explicit behavioral operationalizations, while also showing that labels such as focus or focused work are not inherent properties of the underlying telemetry.

\subsection{Positioning and Evidential Boundaries}

BWCI is an explicitly bounded, constructed behavioral work-continuity proxy derived from recorded computer-interaction telemetry. Keyboard, mouse, application, and switching signals are shared with earlier approaches. Within this thesis, BWCI is distinguished operationally by the definition of the composite score, the prospective formulation of future-level and deterioration outcomes, and its evaluation across temporal, user-transfer, and adaptation scenarios.

The principal distinction instead concerns what is being represented and predicted. Current-interruptibility studies primarily estimate a contemporaneous perceived or operational state. Work-continuance models estimate the duration or remaining extent of an operationally identified interval. Task-transition and resumption approaches characterize changes among tasks, diversions, or breaks. Logs-based focused-work approaches construct work-session or focus-time representations from relationships among recorded tool events. BWCI, by contrast, represents continuity in the observed computer-interaction pattern and defines predictive targets from the future level or operationally defined deterioration of that behavioral proxy. Similarity in input signals therefore does not imply equivalence of construct or target.

The evaluation of BWCI consequently separates several questions that prior work and the methodological literature show to be non-interchangeable. Prediction of later observations for already represented participants addresses a different generalization scenario from transfer to a participant absent from model development, while adaptation using information from that participant introduces a further estimand. Likewise, ranking a deterioration outcome, assessing the quality of predicted probabilities, and evaluating classifications at an operating threshold characterize different properties of a predictive model. Together, these analyses characterize complementary aspects of prediction for the operationalized proxy.

The evidential boundary is equally important. Because BWCI and its prediction targets are operationally constructed from computer-interaction telemetry, successful prediction of future BWCI establishes properties of the proxy and the extent to which its future behavior is predictable from available prior information. It does not independently establish that the proxy measures psychological focus, attention, productivity, perceived interruptibility, or another broader construct. As established by the measurement literature, stronger construct interpretations require evidence relating an operational measure to observations that are not supplied merely by its own definition or internal predictability \parencite{cronbach1955constructValidity,jacobs2021measurement}.

BWCI is situated within a broader family of task-transition, interruptibility, work-continuance, and logs-based behavioral measures. Its score definition and prospective target operationalize continuity in recorded computer interaction, while broader construct claims require independent evidence.
