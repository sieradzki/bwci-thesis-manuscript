\chapter{Background and Related Work}
\label{chap:related-work}

\section{Work Fragmentation, Interruptions, and Resumption}

\subsection{Fragmented Computer-Mediated Work and Task Switching}

Field studies indicate that computer-mediated knowledge work often involves movement among multiple tasks, tools, and broader activity contexts rather than uninterrupted execution of a single task \parencite{gonzalez2004workingSpheres,mark2005fragmentedWork}. González and Mark described these broader contexts as \emph{working spheres}: thematically connected areas of activity that may include multiple events, resources, collaborators, and time horizons. Their observations of analysts, software developers, and managers showed substantial discontinuity in the execution of activities and frequent movement among tools, tasks, and working spheres. These findings establish fragmentation as an observable characteristic of the studied work settings, but they do not provide a universal switching rate for knowledge work.

An application, a task, and a working sphere represent different levels of analysis. A working sphere may involve several documents, software tools, and physical resources, while applications such as email or scheduling tools may be shared among different working spheres. Czerwinski et al. likewise found that participants described task switches at different levels of granularity, and the study discussed the need to organize project-related resources across applications. Application-level telemetry therefore records changes in the observed tool context but does not by itself identify semantic task or working-sphere boundaries \parencite{gonzalez2004workingSpheres,czerwinski2004diary}.

In a detailed observational study of 24 information workers, \textcite{mark2005fragmentedWork} found that 57\% of observed working-sphere segments were interrupted. Most interrupted work was resumed on the same day, but participants engaged in more than two intervening working spheres on average before returning to the interrupted sphere. Fragmentation could therefore involve a sequence of other activities rather than immediate alternation between two tasks. These estimates were obtained in a specific technology-intensive organizational setting and should not be treated as universal rates.

A one-week diary study by \textcite{czerwinski2004diary} recruited 11 experienced Windows users, with one participant excluded from the main analyses for not meeting the stated task-switching criterion. Among the remaining participants, self-initiated switches were the largest recorded category and accounted for 40\% of task switches. Tasks to which participants later returned were longer, involved more documents, and were rated as more difficult to resume than other tasks. The findings distinguish self-directed task management from switches imposed by external interruptions.

The role of self-interruption was examined further by \textcite{dabbish2011selfInterruption}, who analyzed 889 hours of observed task-switching behavior from 36 information workers across three high-technology organizations. Variation in self-interruption was associated with organizational environment, individual differences, and previously experienced external interruptions; participants working in open-office environments self-interrupted at a higher rate. Because the study was observational, these relationships do not establish causal effects.

Taken together, these studies support treating continuity and fragmentation as properties of observable work organization while preserving a strict interpretation boundary. A transition may reflect an interruption, but it may also result from coordination, deliberate reprioritization, self-directed task management, or resumption of ongoing work. The presence of a transition therefore does not by itself establish disruption or an adverse task outcome.

\subsection{Interruption Costs, Resumption, and Attention Residue}

Even when a transition constitutes an interruption rather than ordinary task progression, its consequences depend on when it occurs, how the interrupted task is structured, and which outcome is used to define its cost. Controlled studies have therefore examined several distinct consequences, including completion time, errors, affective responses, and the delay before observable task resumption.

In a laboratory experiment, \textcite{bailey2006attention} compared peripheral tasks presented during primary-task execution with the same peripheral tasks presented at coarse task boundaries. Interruptions during execution increased completion time and errors and produced greater annoyance and anxiety. Within this experiment, interruption timing affected both performance and affective outcomes. The result does not show that application switches in natural computer use identify equivalent task boundaries.

Task structure also affects the operational cost assigned to an interruption. \textcite{iqbal2006interruptionCost} interrupted participants at selected subtask boundaries and defined resumption lag as the interval from closing the peripheral-task window to the first observable action in the resumed primary task. Structural characteristics of the boundary were informative about this outcome, and the resulting model was subsequently evaluated with a different set of laboratory tasks. The study supports distinguishing interruption moments according to the structure of a goal-directed task. Its explicitly modeled subtask boundaries, however, are not interchangeable with application switches or other passive telemetry events.

A different pattern was observed by \textcite{mark2008cost}. Participants in interrupted conditions completed the assigned work faster, with no measured difference in quality, but reported greater stress, frustration, time pressure, and effort. This combination shows why interruption cost cannot be inferred from throughput or output quality alone. It also cautions against treating interruptions as producing a uniform decline in task performance, because behavioral compensation and subjective workload may move in different directions.

A related account is provided by \textcite{leroy2009attentionResidue}: switching away from unfinished work may leave part of a person's attention associated with the previous task and may impair performance on the subsequent task. This evidence does not imply that every task switch, interruption, or application transition produces attention residue.

For the purposes of this thesis, it is useful to distinguish behavioral resumption, resumption of task performance, and cognitive recovery. \emph{Behavioral resumption} is an observable return to activity, such as reopening an application or producing a new input event. \emph{Resumption of task performance} requires task-specific evidence that effective execution has been restored. A resumption lag ending at the first observable action quantifies one aspect of the return process, but does not by itself establish restored performance. \emph{Cognitive recovery} concerns an internal state that computer telemetry does not directly observe. Because most of the evidence reviewed here comes from controlled experiments with explicitly defined tasks and interruptions, it does not transfer automatically to naturalistic interaction logs. Interruption costs depend on timing, task structure, context, and the outcome measured; observable transitions and returns to activity therefore cannot be assigned a universal disruption cost or treated as direct evidence of cognitive recovery.

\section{Interruptibility and Work-Continuance Modeling}

\subsection{Sensing Current Interruptibility}

Research on current interruptibility asks whether a particular moment appears suitable for an interruption. In the studies reviewed here, the construct was operationalized primarily through contemporaneous participant ratings \parencite{fogarty2005interruptibility,zueger2018sensing}. This differs from measuring the cost of an interruption that actually occurs: the former concerns perceived appropriateness at the current moment, whereas the latter concerns consequences such as delay, errors, or workload.

An early field investigation by \textcite{fogarty2005interruptibility} examined whether current interruptibility could be estimated from sensed office context. Four university staff members with similar administrative responsibilities were observed in their normal offices for 14--22 working days, producing 602 hours of recordings. Participants were prompted to rate their current interruptibility on a five-point scale. Target construction was not purely self-reported: 54 prompts that participants clearly heard but did not answer within 30 seconds were assigned to the ``Highly Non-interruptible'' category after individual inspection of the recordings. Audio and video were manually coded to simulate plausible sensors representing social interaction, telephone use, posture, office occupancy, keyboard and mouse activity, and other contextual events. Models based on these manually simulated sensor values exceeded a constant majority-class baseline, and the best reported two-class model outperformed human observers judging the same recordings. Separate analyses examined limited automated audio sensing and hybrid models. The study provided early evidence for sensor-based estimation of current interruptibility, but its four-person, occupationally homogeneous sample and extensive reliance on manually simulated sensors do not support broad population generalization. 

Psychophysiological sensing had also been investigated as a means of estimating perceived interruptibility during software-development work \parencite{zueger2015interruptibility}. Extending this line into a longer naturalistic setting, \textcite{zueger2018sensing} conducted a two-week field study with 13 professional software developers from three companies. Participants periodically rated their current interruptibility on a seven-point scale. Predictor streams included keyboard and mouse interaction, the active application window, calendar information, heart-related measurements, physical activity, and sleep. The comparison of computer-interaction, biometric, and combined feature sets was conducted using participant-specific two-state models. In this comparison, computer-interaction data yielded higher reported accuracy than biometric data alone, while the combined feature set produced the strongest reported result.

Züger et al. also distinguished participant-specific modeling from transfer to a held-out participant. Participant-specific models learned from labeled observations of the same person, whereas a leave-one-participant-out analysis trained on the remaining participants and evaluated transfer to a person excluded from model fitting. Participant-specific models performed better for most participants, but the two analyses represent different intended uses rather than interchangeable estimates of one predictive capability.

Across these studies, target construction relied primarily on contemporaneous participant ratings. Such ratings provide a reference independent of the sensor features, but remain subjective operationalizations shaped by how respondents understand interruptibility and the hypothetical interruption being considered. They are not objective ground truth for interruption cost, psychological focus, or a universal internal state. Keyboard, mouse, application, contextual, and biometric signals served as predictors of the ratings rather than direct measurements of those constructs.

Reported accuracy from these studies is not directly comparable with average precision in the BWCI evaluation because the targets, dichotomization rules, class prevalence, populations, and validation procedures differ. Classification of current interruptibility estimates a reported state at the sampling moment, whereas BWCI concerns prospective prediction of the future level and operationally defined deterioration of a bounded behavioral proxy. Work-continuance modeling introduces a further question: how long the current condition is expected to persist.

\subsection{Predicting Uninterruptible Duration and Work Continuance}

Current interruptibility estimation concerns whether an interruption appears appropriate at a particular moment. A related prospective question is how long an operationally identified interval of lower estimated interruptibility is expected to persist during ongoing computer-mediated work.

An early study by \textcite{iga2015uninterruptibleDuration} examined uninterruptible duration using long-term PC-operation records from four participants. It related duration to application category, keyboard--mouse balance, keyboard and mouse activity rates, application-switching frequency, and time of day. The study provides an early example of duration-oriented analysis using closely related families of computer-interaction signals.

\textcite{shiratori2020workContinuance} developed this direction using naturalistic PC-use records from 13 faculty members and students in a university setting, with individual recording periods extending over several months. The probabilistic work-continuance model represented the probability that work would continue as a function of four feature families: application type, PC-operation activity, the balance between keyboard and mouse activity, and application-switching frequency. These features represented observable characteristics of the current computer-use context and did not require physiological sensing.

The prediction target was derived neither from direct participant reports nor from independently observed interruption events. A separate real-time estimator based on PC-operation information assigned interruptibility to three levels. An \emph{uninterruptible duration} began when the estimated interruptibility level changed from high to medium and ended when it returned to high. Its boundaries were therefore generated algorithmically from related computer telemetry. The authors reported limited classification precision for the underlying estimator in both its three-level and two-level forms and noted that the derived duration could differ substantially from interruptibility as experienced by the participant. The resulting target is consequently best interpreted as an operationally identified work-continuance interval rather than as objective evidence of experienced interruptibility \parencite{shiratori2020workContinuance}.

Evaluation included leave-one-participant-out cross-validation, with positive correlations between predicted and derived durations reported for every held-out participant, although prediction errors varied between individuals. To approximate prediction during an ongoing interval, the authors also used only information available three minutes after its onset. The association remained positive but became weaker, and prediction error increased relative to the full-interval analysis \parencite{shiratori2020workContinuance}.

The study also documents important instrumentation limits. Work performed away from the monitored computer or on an additional device was not represented reliably. A task change involving only a switch between windows of the same application could remain unobserved, while external events such as meetings or visitors could end an interval independently of the recorded PC activity. Substantial errors remained for individual durations. Together with the university-based sample, these limitations constrain the interpretation and generalization of the reported relationships \parencite{shiratori2020workContinuance}.

The study is closely related to BWCI through its use of keyboard and mouse activity, application context, and switching behavior, including a prospective duration-prediction variant. It also evaluates transfer through a leave-one-participant-out procedure. The prediction target and temporal estimand are nevertheless different. Shiratori et al. estimate time to the end of an algorithmically identified uninterruptible interval, whereas BWCI models the future level or operationally defined deterioration of an explicitly bounded behavioral-continuity proxy. Similar predictor families therefore do not imply that the two approaches measure the same construct. These contrasts also raise the broader question of how interruptibility and continuance models behave across individuals and how replication evidence from the wider literature should inform interpretation of small-sample results.

\subsection{User Dependence and Replication Evidence}
Studies of interruptibility and work continuance have reported substantial variation in model performance and participant-level prediction errors. Within-participant prediction using labeled data from the same participant, transfer to a participant excluded from model fitting, and adaptation using participant-specific data therefore represent different evaluation scenarios rather than interchangeable measures of predictive quality. 

This distinction was explicit in the field study by \textcite{zueger2018sensing}. Participant-specific interruptibility models were trained separately for each developer, whereas a general model was evaluated by training on the remaining participants and testing on the held-out person. Participant-specific models performed better for most participants, but not all, and model performance varied across individuals. Learning-curve analyses further showed that the amount of labeled personal data required for an individual model to approach or exceed the general model was participant-dependent. These findings provide evidence of person-dependent predictive relationships, but do not establish that personalization necessarily improves every model or every dimension of predictive performance \parencite{zueger2018sensing}.

Person dependence was also visible in the work-continuance analysis of \textcite{shiratori2020workContinuance}. Although leave-one-participant-out evaluation produced positive correlations between predicted and derived durations for every held-out participant, prediction errors differed considerably between individuals. The authors discussed differences in working style, including keyboard- or mouse-dominant operation and window-management preferences, as possible explanations for this variability rather than as formally established causes \parencite{shiratori2020workContinuance}.

Replication evidence provides a separate reason for caution when interpreting performance from small studies. \textcite{poreba2026replication} conducted an external close replication of the laboratory study reported by \textcite{zueger2015interruptibility}, while documenting deviations from the original study design and procedure. Ten participants completed 60-minute programming sessions, yielding 82 interruptibility observations. The replication retained the five-state perceived-interruptibility target and its binary transformation, used a Naive Bayes classifier, and evaluated accuracy through repeated 10-fold cross-validation against a majority-class ZeroR baseline. For the binary target, accuracy was 8.8 percentage points above the baseline, but variability across validation runs was high and the advantage was not statistically significant. For the five-state target, accuracy was below the majority baseline \parencite{poreba2026replication}.

These findings do not establish that interruptibility is generally unpredictable or invalidate the wider sensing literature. Poreba and Sobernig identified small sample size, class imbalance, and the ad hoc binary transformation of the five-point rating as important threats to validity. The replication's binary label distribution was substantially more balanced than in the original laboratory study, while the five-state label distributions remained imbalanced in both studies. The authors also documented methodological deviations from the original experiment. The replication therefore shows that the apparent strength of predictive results can be sensitive to sample composition, target construction, validation variability, and the baseline used for comparison \parencite{poreba2026replication}.

Taken together, prior work provided evidence that current interruptibility and prospective work continuance can be modeled, while also documenting person-dependent performance and limits on broad generalization from small samples. These findings motivate treating within-participant prediction using labeled data from the same participant, transfer to a previously unseen participant, and personalization as distinct evaluation questions, together with explicit baselines and attention to participant-level heterogeneity and uncertainty. BWCI concerns a different behavioral target, so these studies inform its evaluation principles rather than establish equivalence with interruptibility or work continuance. These findings shift the focus to what computer-interaction telemetry can support as a behavioral measure and where its interpretive limits lie.

\section{Behavioral Telemetry and Proxy Measurement}

\subsection{Computer-Interaction Telemetry and Logs-Based Measures}

Computer-interaction telemetry provides observable records of activity at several levels of abstraction. At the lowest level, a system may record interaction events or aggregate statistics such as keystrokes, mouse actions, active-application use, application switches, or periods without input. These observations have direct technical meaning, such as indicating that an input event occurred or that a particular application was active, but they do not themselves specify the task being performed or the broader significance of the behavior. Additional interpretive meaning is introduced when events are transformed into representations such as activity rhythms, application-context sequences, or operationally defined work sessions.

BEHACOM illustrates a relatively low-level aggregated representation of computer interaction. \textcite{sanchez2020behacom} collected naturalistic computer-use data from twelve users over 55 consecutive days without prescribed tasks. The released dataset represents keyboard, mouse, application-usage, and selected system-resource usage through features aggregated in nominal one-minute windows. Sensitive raw data were processed locally, did not leave the monitored device, and were deleted after feature extraction. The published records are therefore behavioral summaries rather than typed text or the contents of documents. They are associated with user identifiers and are not accompanied by independently observed interruption, focus, or task-performance outcomes. BEHACOM consequently provides a rich substrate for constructing behavioral representations, but the semantic interpretation of those representations must be supplied by a separate operational definition.

Other systems move closer to task-level representations by logging interactions across work tools. \textcite{chen2022workgraph}, for example, used anonymized logs from internal engineering tools to define \emph{personal-focus sessions}. Their operationalization distinguished activities classified as personal focus from interruption events such as work-chat, calendar, or notification interactions. This approach provides an example of how cross-tool logs can be transformed into higher-level work-session constructs, while the resulting meaning depends on the rules used to classify activities and interruptions.

A more explicit examination of this measurement step was provided by \textcite{brown2023focusedWork}. Their logs represented actions from multiple development and communication tools as time-ordered tool-action events; the metric itself relied on tool and action labels rather than user-generated textual content or other metadata. Related actions were represented through learned event similarities and combined into a logs-based \emph{focus time} metric. The authors assessed this metric against self-reported experience at two temporal scales: agreement between focus sessions and diary reports was examined for specific activities, while longitudinal quarterly survey responses were related to focus-time measures aggregated over longer periods.

Importantly, Brown et al.'s preliminary diary study found that nearly identical patterns of logged behavior could be described as flow or non-flow depending on the participant's retrospective experience. This observation contributed to a shift from attempting to identify flow itself toward the broader concept of focused work, which the authors treated as related to, but not equivalent to, flow \parencite{brown2023focusedWork}.

Brown et al. therefore provide a practical example of the distinction between an observable event, a constructed behavioral representation, and an interpretive construct. Tool actions and their temporal relationships are observed; event similarities and focus sessions are constructed from those observations; the interpretation of those sessions as focused work additionally relies on the operational definition and supporting self-report evidence \parencite{brown2023focusedWork}. The same distinction applies more generally to computer-interaction measures. BWCI belongs to this broad family of constructed behavioral representations derived from telemetry, but its meaning cannot follow from keyboard, mouse, or application events alone. Defining such a representation therefore raises a separate measurement question: which construct is supported by the chosen operationalization and by the evidence used to evaluate it?

\subsection{Operationalization and Construct Validity}
The scientific interpretation of a computer-interaction measure depends on its operational definition and on the inferences drawn from it. Observable indicators such as input activity or application switching can be combined into a score or behavioral representation, but interpreting that representation may involve claims that extend beyond what is directly recorded. The distinction between an observed indicator, its operationalization, and the broader construct attributed to it is therefore central to interpreting behavioral telemetry.

The classical account of construct validity by \textcite{cronbach1955constructValidity} addresses this problem for measurements interpreted in terms of attributes that are not directly observed. Cronbach and Meehl argued that construct validation requires connecting a proposed interpretation to a wider network of theoretically expected relationships involving observable evidence. A favorable correlation, prediction, or experimental result may contribute evidence, but construct validity depends on a broader pattern of theoretically expected relationships rather than on any single empirical result.

The same distinction applies to computational measurement. \textcite{jacobs2021measurement} describe theoretical constructs as abstractions that cannot be observed directly and must instead be operationalized through observable properties assumed to be related to them. Operationalization necessarily introduces assumptions and design choices about which indicators are relevant and how they represent the intended construct. A mismatch can consequently arise between the theoretical phenomenon that a computational system is said to measure and what its operational definition actually captures. Making those assumptions explicit is necessary for evaluating the resulting interpretation.

The logs-based study by \textcite{brown2023focusedWork} provides a practical illustration. Brown et al. found that nearly identical patterns of logged activity could correspond to retrospective reports of flow or non-flow. Rather than treating those behavioral patterns as sufficient to distinguish the subjective state, the authors broadened the scope of their metric toward focused work. Their response illustrates how empirical evidence can constrain the interpretation assigned to a constructed behavioral measure rather than simply confirm its initially intended meaning.

BWCI is therefore defined with a deliberately bounded interpretation. It is an operational representation of continuity in recorded computer-interaction behavior and is treated as an observable behavioral work-continuity proxy. In this thesis, the term \emph{proxy} is used to reflect this restricted inferential role: the index summarizes specified aspects of observed behavior without asserting that a broader state is directly observed. Consequently, BWCI is not, by its definition alone, a direct measure of psychological focus, attention, productivity, work quality, perceived interruptibility, or a ground-truth interruption.

This boundary is particularly important when interpreting predictive performance. BWCI is constructed from computer-interaction telemetry, while models predicting future BWCI use current and historical information derived from the same broad measurement substrate. Successful prospective prediction is meaningful evidence that the operationalized behavioral outcome contains temporally predictable structure. It is not independent evidence that the outcome corresponds to a broader construct that is not directly observed in the telemetry. This distinction separates valid prediction of a constructed outcome from independent evidence about what that outcome represents.

Stronger interpretations require evidence beyond the score definition and its internal predictability. Depending on the proposed inference, relevant evidence for a telemetry-based proxy may include relationships with independently collected participant reports, experimentally manipulated conditions, task outcomes, or other observations expected to show theoretically meaningful agreement or divergence \parencite{cronbach1955constructValidity,jacobs2021measurement}. Such evidence may support, refine, or challenge the interpretation attached to an operational measure. An explicit operational definition therefore clarifies the inferential boundary of a behavioral proxy, but neither the definition itself nor its predictability settles its broader meaning. This distinction shifts attention to the limits of the computer-interaction observations from which the representation is constructed.

\subsection{Data and Interpretive Boundaries}
Computer-interaction telemetry is necessarily selective: it records activity visible to the instrumented devices and software rather than the full set of activities that may constitute work. A period without recorded keyboard or mouse input establishes only the absence of those observed input events during the interval. It does not distinguish among activities compatible with little or no input, such as reading, planning, conversations, or meetings, work performed away from the monitored computer, genuine inactivity, or limitations in observation. Assigning any one of these meanings requires information beyond the absence of input itself.

Application telemetry has a related boundary. An active application or an application-switch event provides information about the observed tool context, but does not uniquely identify the task being performed, the relevance of that application to the task, or the significance of a transition. A task may change without a change of application, and an application transition may occur while the same broader task continues. These limitations were visible in the naturalistic study by \textcite{shiratori2020workContinuance}. Switching between separate windows of the same application was not represented by their application-switch measure, activity on an additional computer was absent from the primary-PC record, and meetings, visitors, and other external events could affect work independently of the monitored PC activity. Computer-only sensing therefore provides only a partial view of the context in which observed interaction occurs.

Ambiguity can remain even when a richer pattern of events is available. As discussed previously, \textcite{brown2023focusedWork} found that nearly identical patterns of logged activity could accompany retrospective reports of either flow or non-flow. The observation does not imply that logs are uninformative; rather, it shows that similarity in recorded behavior does not uniquely determine the subjective meaning associated with that behavior.

Minimizing the retention of content-level data limits the explicit content preserved, but does not make interaction telemetry inherently anonymous or non-sensitive. BEHACOM, for example, aggregated locally collected raw interaction data into behavioral features, associated the released feature vectors with user identifiers, transmitted the resulting feature vectors to the server, and deleted the raw data locally after transmission \parencite{sanchez2020behacom}. This design avoids retaining typed text and other raw interaction details in the released feature representation. However, research on behavioral signals shows that information may remain in timing and interaction dynamics themselves. \textcite{balagani2018contextPrivacy} showed, in the setting of continuous keystroke authentication, that keystroke latencies could reveal which application was being used for text entry. Separately, \textcite{zheng2011mouseVerification} demonstrated that fine-grained mouse-movement dynamics could support user verification. These results concern representations and experimental settings different from BWCI; they do not establish that BWCI features reveal application context or permit user identification. They do show more generally that content-minimized interaction telemetry can remain behaviorally distinctive and potentially sensitive.

These observational boundaries constrain the interpretation of any representation constructed from such data. For BWCI, absence of input cannot be equated with absence of work, application-context stability cannot be equated with semantic task stability, and recorded interaction cannot represent activity occurring outside the monitored computer. Similar telemetry may also admit different experiential interpretations, while content minimization does not eliminate the need to treat behavioral data as potentially sensitive. These limitations do not make computer-interaction telemetry unsuitable for behavioral measurement; they specify what conclusions its evidence can support.

Taken together, computer-interaction logs provide useful evidence about observable behavior, but behavioral representations require explicit operational definitions, broader construct interpretations require evidence beyond the representation itself, and the instrumentation determines what remains observable or ambiguous. These principles justify the deliberately restricted interpretation of BWCI as a proxy of continuity in recorded computer interaction rather than a direct measure of a broader work or psychological state.

\section{Evaluation Principles for Temporal and Person-Dependent Prediction}

Model performance is meaningful only relative to the prediction scenario represented by the evaluation design. This is particularly important when observations are ordered in time and multiple records originate from the same individuals. A procedure that estimates performance on later observations from already represented participants addresses a different question from one that estimates transfer to a participant absent from model development.

For prospective prediction, the direction of evaluation should correspond to the direction of the intended inference. \textcite{saeb2017useCase} make this point in the different domain of clinical and mobile-sensing prediction: the appropriate validation scheme depends on the use case, and prediction of future states for an already observed person can be evaluated by separating that person's records in time. Random record-wise splitting instead mixes repeated records across partitions and therefore does not by itself establish either later-time prediction or transfer to a new participant. In temporal behavioral data, the relevant question is consequently whether information available before a prediction point supports prediction of a later outcome. Incorporating a longer history through lagged features or a sequence representation changes the information supplied to the model, but does not by itself establish superior prediction relative to a simpler representation.

Person-level generalization introduces a separate distinction. When records from a participant are available during model development, evaluation on that participant's later observations can exploit stable person-specific regularities. Unseen-participant evaluation instead asks whether relationships learned from other participants transfer without prior data from the target person. Personalization or adaptation asks a third question because observations from the target participant are deliberately introduced into model fitting or adaptation. Saeb et al. show why record-wise and subject-wise validation can yield substantially different estimates when repeated person-level observations are present, while the interruptibility study by \textcite{zueger2018sensing} provides a domain-specific example in which participant-specific and held-out-participant models produced different performance patterns \parencite{saeb2017useCase}. None of these scenarios provides an interchangeable estimate of a single notion of general model quality, and personalization should be evaluated rather than assumed to improve prediction.

The performance measure must likewise reflect the target distribution and the property of interest. \textcite{saito2015precisionRecall} show that, under class imbalance, accuracy may conceal poor performance on the minority class and that ROC-space can obscure practically important changes that become visible in precision--recall space. Precision is directly affected by the number of false positive predictions relative to true positive predictions, making the precision--recall perspective particularly informative when the positive outcome is uncommon. This motivates evaluating rare-outcome ranking with measures that emphasize the positive class while reporting prevalence and threshold-dependent behavior separately. The exact numerical summary used for a precision--recall curve is an implementation choice and should not be inferred from Saito and Rehmsmeier's argument alone.

Ranking, probability calibration, and thresholded decisions address further distinct questions. Ranking or discrimination concerns whether cases with the positive outcome tend to receive higher model scores than negative cases. Calibration concerns whether predicted probabilities correspond to observed outcome frequencies. \textcite{niculescu2005goodProbabilities} demonstrate empirically that strong classification accuracy or ROC-area performance does not guarantee well-calibrated probability estimates, and that the quality of predicted probabilities can therefore require separate evaluation. Converting a score or probability into a binary decision introduces an additional operating threshold: performance at that point depends not only on the ordering or calibration of predictions, but also on where the threshold is placed. A ranking metric, a calibration assessment, and threshold-specific classification results therefore characterize different properties of the same predictive system rather than substitutes for one another.

For BWCI, these principles motivate keeping several evaluation questions separate: prospective prediction of later proxy values for represented participants, transfer to a held-out participant, adaptation using participant-specific information, ranking of a deterioration outcome, probability calibration, and classification at a chosen operating threshold. These dimensions are not treated as alternative names for overall model quality, nor as methodological novelties of BWCI. They define distinct estimands and predictive properties whose interpretation depends on the temporal setting, the relationship between training and evaluation participants, the class distribution, and the form in which model output is evaluated.
